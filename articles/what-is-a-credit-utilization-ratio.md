When lenders evaluate someone’s creditworthiness, they look at several factors to determine how safely that person manages debt. One of the most important metrics they consider is the **credit utilization ratio**. 

Simply put, this ratio measures how much revolving credit a person is currently using compared to the total amount of credit available to them. It is expressed as a percentage and applies specifically to revolving credit accounts, such as credit cards and personal lines of credit, rather than installment loans like mortgages or auto loans.

Understanding how this ratio is calculated and how it affects credit health can help individuals make more informed financial decisions.

---

## How to Calculate Your Credit Utilization Ratio

Calculating this ratio involves a simple mathematical formula. To find the percentage, divide the total outstanding balance on all revolving accounts by the total credit limit across those same accounts, then multiply by 100.

**Credit Utilization Ratio** = (Total Outstanding Balances ÷ Total Credit Limits) × 100

Credit scoring models typically look at two different types of utilization:
1. **Per-card utilization:** The ratio on each individual credit card.
2. **Overall utilization:** The combined ratio across all open credit card accounts.

### An Illustrative Example

To see how this works in practice, imagine someone has two credit cards with the following limits and balances:

| Credit Card | Credit Limit | Current Balance | Individual Utilization Ratio |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Card A** | $2,000 | $800 | 40% |
| **Card B** | $3,000 | $400 | 13.3% |
| **Total** | **$5,000** | **$1,200** | **24% (Overall)** |

In this scenario, the individual's overall credit utilization ratio is **24%** ($1,200 divided by $5,000). However, the utilization on Card A is **40%**. Because credit scoring models often look at both individual and total utilization, having a high balance on a single card can still influence a credit score negatively, even if the overall utilization remains low.

## Why Does Credit Utilization Matter?

Credit utilization is one of the most influential components of a credit score. In major credit scoring models, such as FICO® and VantageScore®, this metric typically falls under the "amounts owed" category, which generally accounts for about 30% of a person's total score. Only payment history carries more weight.

Lenders pay close attention to this ratio because it serves as an indicator of financial risk:
* **High utilization** (using a large portion of available credit) can signal to lenders that a borrower is overextended or relying too heavily on credit to cover daily expenses.
* **Low utilization** suggests that a borrower uses credit sparingly and is highly likely to manage additional debt responsibly.

---

## What Is a "Good" Credit Utilization Ratio?

A common rule of thumb in personal finance is to keep credit utilization below 30%. While staying under 30% is a helpful baseline, it is not a hard-and-fast rule where credit health suddenly plummets at 31% or becomes perfect at 29%. 

Generally, when it comes to credit utilization, lower is better. Individuals with the highest credit scores often maintain an overall utilization ratio in the single digits—typically below 10%. 

It is also worth noting that having a 0% utilization ratio is not always ideal. If all credit card balances show $0 at the time the credit bureaus pull the data, the scoring model may interpret this as inactivity, which can occasionally result in a slightly lower score than having a very small, active balance (such as 1% to 2%) that is paid off in full each month.

## Strategies for Managing Credit Utilization

For those looking to lower or maintain their credit utilization ratio, several practical approaches can be used.

### 1. Paying Balances Before the Statement Closing Date
Credit card issuers typically report account balances to the credit bureaus once a month, usually on the "statement closing date" (which is different from the payment due date). Paying down the balance before this closing date ensures that a lower balance is reported to the bureaus, which keeps the recorded utilization ratio low.

### 2. Requesting a Credit Limit Increase
If a credit card issuer increases a card's limit while the cardholder keeps their spending at the same level, the utilization ratio will automatically drop. For example, if a card has a $500 balance on a $1,000 limit, the utilization is 50%. If the limit is increased to $2,000, that same $500 balance represents a 25% utilization ratio.

### 3. Spreading Purchases Across Multiple Cards
Instead of putting a large purchase on a single card, some consumers choose to distribute their spending across multiple accounts to prevent any single card's utilization ratio from spiking.

### 4. Keeping Unused Accounts Open
Closing an old, unused credit card removes that card’s credit limit from the overall pool of available credit. This reduces the total credit limit, which can cause the overall utilization ratio to rise overnight. Keeping zero-balance cards open and active with occasional small purchases can help maintain a higher overall credit limit.
