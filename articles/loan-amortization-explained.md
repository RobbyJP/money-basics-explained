A loan's monthly payment looks simple, but behind it sits a schedule called amortization — the gradual repayment of a loan through fixed payments where interest is front-loaded. Understanding it explains two things most borrowers miss: why the balance barely moves in the early years, and why small extra payments are disproportionately powerful. This guide covers the mechanics, the Indonesia-specific (KPR) reality, and how to use the calculator above.

## How a fixed monthly payment is built

For a fixed-rate loan the bank derives one payment that, over the full term, exactly repays the principal plus interest. The standard formula:

> Payment = P × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n)

Where `P` is the loan amount, `i` the monthly interest rate, and `n` the number of monthly payments.

Each month your payment is split in two:

- **Interest portion** = current balance × monthly rate
- **Principal portion** = payment − interest

Because the interest portion shrinks as the balance falls, the principal portion grows with every month. Early payments are mostly interest; late payments are mostly principal. That is the amortization curve — and it is why paying off "early" in a loan's life saves so much.

### Worked example

A $300,000 loan at 9.5% per year for 15 years:

- Monthly payment: about $3,132
- Total paid over 15 years: about $563,800
- Total interest: about **$263,800** — 88% of the loan amount

The calculator above reproduces these numbers and shows the balance curve month by month.

## Why the balance seems stuck

In year one of that same loan, roughly $2,375 of each payment is interest and only about $757 reaches the principal. After five years you will have made about $188,000 in payments — yet the balance will still be above $240,000. This is not the bank being unfair; it is simply how fixed payments with front-loaded interest work. The turning point comes after roughly two-thirds of the term, when principal payments start to dominate.

## The superpower of extra payments

Add $500 to the monthly payment in the example above and two things happen:

- **The loan is paid off in about 11 years instead of 15** — four years earlier.
- **Total interest drops from about $263,800 to about $188,700** — roughly $75,000 saved.

Extra payments attack the most expensive part of the loan (the interest that would have been charged on the early principal) and shorten the term directly. On high-rate consumer loans the "guaranteed return" of an extra payment often beats what the same money would earn invested.

## KPR specifics in Indonesia

Most Indonesian mortgages use a **floating rate** that the bank can adjust (often every quarter), so the payment is not truly fixed. Practical consequences:

- **Re-run the calculator whenever the rate changes** — a 1 percentage point rise on a 15-year KPR adds tens of millions in interest.
- **Do not confuse the intro rate** — low first-year promotional rates (often 2–3%) revert to market rates afterwards; the "discount" period is marketing, not a discount.
- **The 100% financing trap** — loans that cover the full property price carry higher rates and fees, increasing both payment and total interest.
- **Extra payments during early years are most valuable** — this is exactly when floating-rate KPR borrowers have the least spare cash, so even occasional lump sums (THR, bonus) moved to the loan beat most alternatives.

## A decision framework for extra payments

Use the calculator above and compare:

1. **Cash-flow impact:** can the extra payment survive a rate hike without breaking your budget?
2. **Comparison against alternatives:** extra payment beats low-yield savings, usually loses to high-yield investments over long horizons — but is a *guaranteed* return.
3. **Emergency fund first:** do not over-pay a loan until you have a separate emergency cushion; a loan cannot be reborrowed on demand without cost.
