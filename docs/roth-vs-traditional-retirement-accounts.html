Money Basics Explained

When planning for the future, choosing where to save for retirement is one of the most important decisions an investor can make. Most retirement plans—including Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) and workplace 401(k) plans—come in two primary styles: traditional and Roth.

While both accounts offer powerful tax advantages to help savings grow over time, they handle taxes in opposite ways. The fundamental difference lies in when the tax break is claimed: today or during retirement.

How Traditional Retirement Accounts Work

Traditional retirement accounts are funded with pre-tax dollars. This means that any money contributed to the account is deducted from the saver's taxable income for that year, reducing their current tax bill.

For example, if an individual earns $70,000 a year and contributes $6,000 to a traditional IRA, their taxable income for that year drops to $64,000. This immediate tax savings allows the investor to keep more of their hard-earned money today.

However, this tax benefit is a deferral, not a cancellation. The money in the account grows tax-deferred, meaning no taxes are owed on investment growth while the funds remain in the account. When the investor withdraws the money in retirement, those withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income at whatever their tax rate happens to be at that time.

How Roth Retirement Accounts Work

Roth retirement accounts flip the tax equation. Contributions to a Roth account are made with after-tax dollars. There is no upfront tax deduction, meaning the saver pays regular income tax on that money today before it goes into the account.

For example, if someone in a moderate tax bracket contributes $6,000 to a Roth IRA, they do not get to write that contribution off their current year's taxes.

The primary benefit of a Roth account comes down the road. Because income taxes were already paid on the contributions, the money grows entirely tax-free. When the investor reaches retirement age (typically age 59½ or older) and begins withdrawing the funds, both the original contributions and all the accumulated investment earnings can be withdrawn completely tax-free.

At-a-Glance: Roth vs. Traditional Accounts

Feature Traditional Accounts Roth Accounts
Tax Break Timing Immediate (on contributions) Future (on withdrawals)
Contribution Type Pre-tax dollars After-tax dollars
Retirement Withdrawals Taxed as ordinary income 100% Tax-free

Key Factors in the Roth vs. Traditional Decision

Choosing between these two paths often depends on an individual's current financial situation and their expectations for the future.

Current vs. Future Tax Brackets

The primary math problem behind this choice involves comparing a person's current tax bracket with their expected tax bracket in retirement: * Higher tax bracket today: For individuals currently in their peak earning years who expect to be in a lower tax bracket during retirement, traditional accounts tend to be highly efficient. By taking the tax deduction now, they avoid paying high taxes today and instead pay lower taxes later. * Higher tax bracket in the future: For young professionals, students, or those currently in entry-level positions, their current tax rate may be the lowest it will ever be. In this scenario, paying taxes now through a Roth account is often highly advantageous, allowing them to lock in a low tax rate and enjoy tax-free growth for decades.

Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

Traditional accounts require savers to start taking annual withdrawals (known as Required Minimum Distributions, or RMDs) once they reach a certain age, regardless of whether they actually need the money. This ensures the government eventually collects its deferred tax revenue. Roth IRAs, on the other hand, do not have RMDs during the lifetime of the original owner, offering greater flexibility for estate planning and wealth preservation.

Tax Diversification

Because predicting future tax laws and personal tax brackets decades in advance is difficult, many savers choose a strategy called tax diversification. This involves contributing to both traditional and Roth accounts. Holding both types of accounts allows a retiree to strategically withdraw from different tax buckets in retirement to manage their annual taxable income and minimize their overall tax burden.

Planning for the Future

Both traditional and Roth accounts are excellent vehicles for building long-term wealth. The decision does not have to be an all-or-nothing choice. Evaluating one's current income level, career trajectory, and need for tax flexibility can help clarify which account type makes the most sense for a specific financial strategy.
